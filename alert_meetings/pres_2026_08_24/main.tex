\begin{frame}{Known issue}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For complete signals, the time of arrival is extracted at half the amplitude
        \item For saturated signals, the true amplitude is not accessible
            \begin{itemize}
                \item the reconstructed time is \textbf{currently} under-estimated
                \item some hits may not pass selection cuts and tracks can be lost
            \end{itemize}
        \item This study aimed to apply a special treatment to these signals
    \end{itemize}

    \centering
    \vspace{0.2cm}

    \begin{figure}

    \end{figure}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Observable}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item We found that the slope \footnote{Maximum deviation of the signal between to consecutive samples} of the signal is linearly correlated to the amplitude of the signal
        \item In the saturation regime, one can then use the slope to estimate the amplitude of the signal
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{equation*}
        \bm{ADC}_{corr} = f(\bm{slope})
    \end{equation*}

\begin{frame}{Time reconstruction}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Once we have a reasonable estimate of the true amplitude, we can recompute the time at half amplitude
            \begin{itemize}
                \item The error of the estimation is well known and is propagated to the reconstructed time 
                \item The big slope of saturated signals make the error on the time very minimal
                \item The error on the time can be propagated to the DOCA and provided to the Kalman Filter
            \end{itemize}
        %\item 
    \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Results - Effect of the ADC correction}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Before correction
            \begin{itemize}
                \item We had an accumulation of small time around 3600 ADC
                \item The distribution in the saturation region was unrelated to the distribution in the adjacent region, e.g. just below 3400 ADC
            \end{itemize}
        \item After correction
            \begin{itemize}
                \item The distribution is smoother, no apparent bias is observable
                \item Quantitatively, we move from 2244 reconstructed tracks to 2436 over 239852 selected trigger events in run 21476, on 4He at 10.6 GeV, so a gain of 8.55\%
            \end{itemize}
        %\item Quantitatively, we move from 2244 reconstructed tracks to 2436 over 239852 selected trigger events in run 21476, so a gain of 8.55\%
    \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Validation - Comparison with ToT}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Our slope-based approach has been compared with the usual time over threshold (ToT) method
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Principle: when the signal reaches the saturation plateau, the time spent above the threshold increases with the amplitude of the signal
                \item We select complete waveforms between 1800 ADC and 3500 ADC
                \item We compare their ToT and their slope to their known amplitude
            \end{itemize}
        \item \textit{\textbf{(i)}} The ToT versus ADC is not linear, \textit{\textbf{(ii)}} it is more precise than the slope at low ADC, \textit{\textbf{(iii)}} but the resolution increases with the ADC while it is almost constant in the slope method
    \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Validation - Comparison with ToT}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Due to the linear behavior of the ADC resolution based on the ToT, the resolution at 6000 ADC is expected to be about 685 ADC.
        \item In contrast, the slope method is more consistent as the maximum resolution is about 250 ADC
    \end{itemize}
